Why is my compost pile not heating up?

A compost pile stays cool when microbial activity is limited or when the heat being produced escapes too quickly. The same symptom can have several causes.

BioFarmLab·22/10/2026·updated 11/11/2026
Polski →Italiano →Español →Français →Čeština →Deutsch →

A compost pile stays cool when its microorganisms work more slowly than they could, or when the heat they produce escapes faster than it builds up. Temperature is an outcome. The source of the heat is biological activity, so the thermometer reflects how good the conditions are for that activity. Several different causes can produce the same cool reading, which is why the number alone cannot settle the diagnosis.

My compost pile is not getting hot. What should I check?

Look inside the pile and compare its moisture, structure, ingredients and size with the temperature reading. These observations help distinguish causes that produce the same cool reading.

  • Dry material: check moisture inside the pile as well as at the surface. A lack of water limits microbial activity.
  • Wet, compacted material: check whether spaces remain for air between the particles. More watering can make this oxygen problem worse.
  • The mix: record which materials went into the pile. Ingredients provide food for organisms and create the structure that allows air to move.
  • A small or spread-out pile: consider heat loss as well as microbial activity. The pile needs to retain the heat its organisms produce.

How do I use compost thermometer readings?

Record the date, pile and ambient temperatures, moisture observations, turning and other changes. Readings taken over time show what happened before the pile cooled and after you changed something. The Compost Log keeps these observations together; the Compost Calculator helps plan pile volume, ingredient proportions and an estimated carbon-to-nitrogen ratio.

Where the heat in a pile comes from

Heat appears during aerobic decomposition, the breakdown of organic matter in the presence of oxygen. Microorganisms, mainly bacteria and fungi, take in organic material as a source of energy and building blocks. They consume oxygen while doing so, and part of the energy they release becomes heat. Cornell Waste Management Institute describes this in its material on the chemistry of composting. The principle resembles an animal’s body heat: warmth is a byproduct of metabolism, produced here by an enormous number of tiny organisms working at once.

A pile therefore warms up when three things coincide. The microorganisms have suitable amounts of water and air, they have material they can break down, and the heat they generate stays inside the pile. When one of these falls short, heat either fails to form in sufficient quantity or has no way to accumulate. These three areas are the broad limits that decide whether a pile gets going.

Water and air share the same spaces

The material in a pile is full of small voids called pores. Each pore holds either water or air, and microorganisms need both. Water forms thin films around particles, which is where the organisms live and take up nutrients. Air supplies the oxygen that aerobic decomposition runs on.

Because water and air occupy the same spaces, a change in one always shifts the other. In a pile that is too wet or too compacted, water fills the pores and oxygen runs short. Aerobic microorganisms slow down, and heat production falls with them. In a pile that is too dry, the moisture that life depends on is missing, so biological activity fades even though air is plentiful. Both piles end up cool, yet the mechanisms behind them are opposites.

Material is both fuel and structure

Microorganisms break down whatever they are given, and the composition of the material affects how quickly and efficiently that happens. In a pile the material plays two roles at once. It is the food from which organisms draw energy and nutrients, and it forms the physical framework that determines how pores are arranged, and with them airflow and water distribution.

For this reason the material can limit the process even when other conditions look favorable. Composition governs what the microorganisms have available, and structure governs how everything is laid out in space. Both influence the rate of decomposition and, through it, the amount of heat released. This area is often the hardest to judge from the outside, because material that looks ordinary can behave quite differently inside a pile than its appearance suggests.

The pile has to hold on to what it makes

The third area is physics rather than microbiology. Heat is generated throughout the volume of the pile and lost through its surface, so size and shape determine how much stays in the core and how much passes to the surroundings. A pile with too little mass or an unfavorable shape can host perfectly healthy microorganisms and still never reach a temperature that registers on a thermometer, because losses keep pace with production.

This mechanism is easy to overlook because it feels like a matter of construction rather than biology. Yet heat that is not retained leaves no trace in a measurement, and the observer sees a “dead” pile while decomposition continues inside. University of Minnesota Extension’s guidance on building a compost pile, written for horse carcass composting, likewise treats the size and construction of the pile as relevant to how the process unfolds.

Why temperature alone does not identify the problem

Temperature is a signal that more or less is happening in the pile. It gathers the effects of all three areas into a single number: water and air, material, and the capacity to retain heat. From one reading it is impossible to tell which of them dominates, much as a fever tells a doctor that something is wrong without saying what.

A pile that is too wet, too dry, built from problematic material, or simply too small can show a very similar reading. Each situation calls for a different response, and some call for opposite ones. A temperature reading becomes valuable as one element of a wider picture, when it is read alongside an assessment of moisture, structure, and how the pile was built. On its own it can mislead.

Why random fixes can make things worse

The natural reaction to a cool pile is to change something and hope. A correction works only when it targets the actual cause. If excess water is the limit and the pile gets watered further, the problem deepens. If the cause is a limited capacity to retain heat, no adjustment to moisture will repair it.

Making several changes at once adds another difficulty. When the pile warms afterward, there is no telling which change worked, and when it stays cool, there is no telling which one might have done harm. Managing a pile well means first identifying which area is limiting the process and acting second. The order of those steps and the way each area is assessed form a method that goes beyond what a single article can lay out.
