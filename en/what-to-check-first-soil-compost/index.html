Soil & microbiology

Soil or compost problem? Find the cause before you add another product

Field observation, chemistry, biological assessment and compost process signals provide different information. Start with the question you need answered.

BioFarmLab·22/10/2026·updated 11/11/2026
Deutsch →Polski →Italiano →Español →Français →Čeština →

Crops grow poorly even though the field was fertilized. A compost pile stays cool after weeks of work. After rain, water stands on one part of the property and soaks away on another. Each of these situations invites a quick purchase: more fertilizer, a microbial product, another load of compost. Whether that purchase helps depends on whether it reaches the actual cause. The kinds of information available for narrowing the cause each answer a different question, and knowing which is which makes the next decision much better.

One symptom, several possible causes

A symptom narrows the search area without naming the culprit. Poor growth despite fertilization can come from soil structure and compaction that restrict root development. It can also relate to pH or to the biological processes that drive nutrient cycling. In that case, extra fertilizer delivers another portion of something the soil already holds, while the limitation sits elsewhere.

Standing water after rain points toward structure, compaction and the balance of water and air in the soil, and a nutrient shortage moves to the background. A cool compost pile may reflect limited microbial access to carbon and nitrogen, problems with oxygen or moisture, or particles that are too large. A sharp, pungent smell in compost or manure often accompanies anaerobic conditions, which develop when oxygen runs short. Cornell Composting notes that a single process signal can have more than one cause, so a smell tells you where to look next and leaves the verdict open.

Soil as a physical, chemical and biological system

Soil works as a whole in which three layers of description influence one another. Structure and compaction govern how water and air move through it. Chemistry determines which nutrients and what pH a root meets. Biology, the living part of the soil, affects soil structure, water availability, the decomposition of organic matter and nutrient cycling, according to the USDA NRCS Soil Biology Primer.

The Primer describes the soil food web as a community that includes bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, arthropods and earthworms, all taking part in processes that matter for plant growth. Because the three layers interact, each method of observation covers only one of them. A fuller picture emerges when several are placed side by side.

What the field tells you

Observation on site shows how the whole system behaves. It reveals how water infiltrates and where it pools, what the soil structure looks like, and whether compaction is present. It also shows how roots look and develop, what condition the plants are in, and whether different zones of the field behave differently.

Differences between zones are especially informative. If plants in one part of a field grow clearly worse than in the neighboring part, and both received the same fertilizer, the search narrows to whatever separates those zones. The field therefore indicates the location and character of a limitation: water-related, structural, or tied to roots. When the cause lies in soil chemistry or biology, it takes the appropriate laboratory methods to examine it.

What a chemical test measures

According to Penn State Extension, a standard soil test is used to evaluate fertility, including pH and selected nutrients. The result describes specific parameters measured by a specific method.

That result depends on the laboratory, the method, the extraction, the scope of the package and the way data are reported. Penn State emphasizes that no single universal report exists that looks the same across laboratories. Sample reports cover pH and selected nutrients, and their interpretation is tied to the method and to crop response. A number on a report gains meaning from its context: which package was ordered, how it was run, and how it relates to the crop being grown. Chemical testing answers questions about pH and about whether selected nutrients fall within a range suited to a given crop.

What biological assessment adds

The BioFarmLab laboratory examines samples under the microscope. The microscope is an observation tool, and a person interprets the image. The assessment covers the presence and abundance of the main groups (bacteria, fungi, protists and nematodes) as well as their proportions, including the bacteria-to-fungi ratio. Such a description shows which levels of the living soil system are present and how numerous each group is.

A pattern has repeated in the samples that have reached BioFarmLab so far. Samples from agricultural soils showed a strongly simplified biological picture. Bacterial dominance was the most common finding, the fungal part was poorly developed, and further levels of the food web were missing. The laboratory also received biologically good compost samples and material from well-managed gardens.

This is a first-party observation from the laboratory, with clear limits. Samples sent to BioFarmLab are not a random sample of farmland, so they support no statistical claims about fields in any region and explain no causes. Their value is practical: they illustrate what the biology of farmed soil can look like and why it is worth examining before planning changes.

Two soils with similar chemistry

Picture two soils with nearly the same pH and similar results for selected nutrients. On a chemical report they look almost identical. Under the microscope, the first may show a richer system with bacteria, fungi, protists and nematodes all present. The second may be biologically simplified, with bacterial dominance, a weak fungal component and no higher levels of the food web.

These are two different starting points. The first soil has a broader representation of the groups involved in decomposing organic matter and cycling nutrients, while the second has fewer such participants. The same intervention, for example introducing compost or a biological preparation, therefore lands in systems with different capabilities and can mean something different in each. Chemistry describes which nutrients and what pH a plant encounters, and biology shows which groups of organisms make up the living part of the soil. The two descriptions complement each other.

A baseline, and how many things change at once

A biological assessment before any changes creates a baseline. A later sample, collected in a comparable way, lets you follow the direction of change: whether the system is becoming richer, remains simplified, or whether proportions between groups are shifting. Without a starting picture, a later result has nothing to be compared with, because you cannot tell whether what you see came from your actions or was the condition all along.

Two measurements establish the direction of change, but they cannot by themselves prove what caused it. That takes reasoning and knowledge of what happened in the field in between.

Attribution matters here. If you change fertilization, add compost, apply a product and alter cultivation all at once, any later improvement or decline will be hard to assign to one of those changes. A working farm or homestead cannot always alter one factor at a time, so the practical goal is to limit the number of simultaneous changes and to keep the baseline. The fewer factors that shift together, the easier the effect is to interpret. Notes on what was done and when become part of the data.

Compost as a changing process

Compost is a biological process that changes over time. According to Cornell Composting, microorganisms decompose the material using, among other things, carbon and nitrogen, and the availability of these elements influences the rate of decomposition. Oxygen is essential for the aerobic process, and when it runs short the process can shift toward anaerobic conditions and produce unwelcome odors. The structure of the material and the size of its particles determine how easily microorganisms can reach it.

Temperature, smell, moisture and the behavior of the pile are therefore signals about the state of the process, and each takes on meaning next to the others and next to the pile’s history. A cool pile with good structure and a neutral smell calls for a different reading than a cool pile with a sharp smell and a compacted texture. A series of observations over time carries more information than a single reading on one day.

Matching each source to its question

Each source of information answers a different question. The field indicates the area where a limitation may lie: water, structure, roots. Chemical analysis describes pH and selected nutrients according to the method of the chosen laboratory. Biological assessment shows which groups of organisms are present, in what amounts and proportions. Observation of compost reveals the state of the process at a given moment. Each covers a fragment of the system, and confidence in a conclusion grows when several independent observations point in the same direction.

A result is one element of a decision process, and its value lies in helping answer a concrete question: does the planned change make sense in this place, on this soil, at this moment? A well-made decision also accounts for what the data do not yet settle, and leaves room for a later comparison.

Combining these elements into one system takes practice: gathering field observations, reading a chemical result, understanding a biological picture and tracking compost over time.
