What does the app add to the course?

The lessons and tools are parts of the same package. Soil Radar brings your field observations together; Compost Calculator helps plan a mix; Compost Log keeps the pile’s measurements and history. The practical value is the connection: you have somewhere to work with your own observations and a course to help you understand them.

Compost Calculator brings pile dimensions, material quantities, an estimated carbon-to-nitrogen ratio and the proportions of high-nitrogen, green and brown materials into one view. Its charts compare your mix with the reference used in the course tools. Soil Radar displays five aspects of your soil observations together, while Compost Log keeps temperature records and the pile’s history. You can work with your own materials and measurements while returning to the lessons that explain their meaning.

Looking for an app or a calculator first?

The tools provide another way into the same programme. Compost Calculator helps plan a pile and its recipe. Compost Log keeps temperature, moisture and process history together. Soil Radar records observations of a named plot or field so you can return to them over time.

The English-language course explains the biology and the work behind those tools. Both the course and the app belong to the same Living Soil Shortcut package from BioFarmLab.

How to choose a practical programme for your own ground

Living Soil Shortcut is BioFarmLab’s English-language course in soil biology and composting. The same course and app package is available on BioFarmLab’s English course page.

A practical soil biology course should teach you to assess your own ground, make and use biologically rich compost, and check the results. Its value lies in the decisions you can make in your garden or on your farm.

Individual guides answer individual questions. Starting a growing project or changing farm management also requires a sequence: what to check first, where to begin recovery and how to recognise whether your actions help the soil.

A programme should connect the whole process

Look for three connected skills.

First, recognising soil condition. Plants, roots, structure and the behaviour of water provide a basis for action. Start with our article on identifying soil and compost problems.

Second, working with compost: choosing materials, managing the process, recognising difficulties and applying the result. Good teaching connects these stages, helping use available materials and organise the work. One example is choosing how to use finished compost.

Third, checking change. After acting, return to observation and compare soil conditions. Future decisions then draw on your own experience too.

Tools should support decisions

A compost diary is a useful example. A single temperature reading shows the present moment. Successive readings alongside dates of work reveal how the process unfolds.

Recording turning or moistening lets later readings be related to specific actions. This helps reconstruct events and investigate change. Temperature does not replace other observations, but stops being an isolated number.

Living Soil Shortcut includes a compost diary, a soil assessment radar and a compost materials calculator. They come as spreadsheets, with an app also available for phone use.

Start with your own ground and resources

A microscope is not needed to begin learning. Start with observation and simple field tests. Biological testing can later reveal organisms in a sample when that information is needed.

A programme should also account for scale. A few beds and a farm differ in material, equipment and time requirements. Understanding the process helps adapt the approach.

We run our own farm, examine soil in our laboratory and design compost machinery. We therefore teach work we do ourselves: preparing materials, managing composting and applying compost in crops.
